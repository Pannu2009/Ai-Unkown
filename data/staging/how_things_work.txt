How things work. Machines and systems, part by part.

Human: how does a smartphone work, part by part
Brain: The system-on-chip (SoC) is the brain — CPU cores for tasks, GPU for graphics, all on one silicon square. RAM holds what's running now; flash storage keeps files without power. The touchscreen is a grid of capacitors sensing your finger's electric field. The cellular radio talks to towers, WiFi and Bluetooth are separate radios. The battery is lithium-ion. Sensors — accelerometer, gyroscope, GPS, light, proximity — tell it about the world. The operating system (Android/iOS) manages it all, giving each app its own sandbox.
Human: how does a touchscreen know where you touch
Brain: Capacitive sensing. The glass has a grid of transparent electrodes holding a tiny electric charge. Your finger conducts electricity, so it steals a little charge where it touches. The controller scans the grid thousands of times per second, finds the disturbed spot, and reports coordinates. That is why gloves block it (no conductivity) and why it supports multi-touch — each finger disturbs its own spot.
Human: how does the internet actually work
Brain: Your request leaves as packets — small chunks with addresses. Your router sends them to your ISP, which hands them across a web of undersea cables, fiber lines, and exchanges. DNS translates names (google.com) to numbers (IP addresses). Routers read each packet's destination and forward it hop by hop. TCP numbers the packets and re-requests lost ones; your device reassembles them in order. The whole trip often takes under a tenth of a second.
Human: what is an IP address
Brain: A device's numerical address on a network. IPv4 looks like 192.168.1.1 (4 billion possible — we ran out). IPv6 is longer (340 trillion trillion trillion possible). Your home shares one public IP; the router sorts traffic to devices by port numbers. Websites see your public IP — that is how region blocks and rough location work.
Human: what is WiFi
Brain: Radio networking on unlicensed frequencies (2.4 GHz travels far through walls, 5/6 GHz is faster but shorter range). Your router broadcasts a network name; devices join with a password and get local IP addresses. WiFi is just the last hop — the router still needs its wired or cellular link to the internet. Slow WiFi is usually congestion (too many networks on one channel) or distance, not your internet plan.
Human: how does fiber optic internet work
Brain: Data as light pulses through hair-thin glass strands. Light bounces inside the fiber by total internal reflection, traveling kilometers with little loss. One fiber carries terabits — your whole neighborhood's traffic on a thread. Lasers flash billions of times per second; the receiver converts flashes back to bits. That is why fiber beats copper: light is faster and cleaner than electricity over distance.
Human: how does the electricity grid work
Brain: Power plants generate AC at high voltage (hundreds of kilovolts) — high voltage means low current for the same power, and low current means less heat lost in wires. Transmission towers carry it across countries. Substations step it down in stages: to tens of kilovolts for towns, to 400/230 or 120 volts for homes via pole transformers. The grid must balance supply and demand every second — too much or too little and frequency drifts, damaging equipment. Grid operators watch it like hawks.
Human: where does your home electricity come from, the full path
Brain: A generator spins (steam from coal/gas/nuclear, falling water, or wind turning blades). Its AC goes through a step-up transformer to transmission voltage. High-voltage lines carry it hundreds of kilometers. A substation steps it down for regional distribution. Pole or pad transformers step it to household voltage. Your meter measures kilowatt-hours; the breaker panel splits it into circuits with breakers that trip on overload. Flip a switch and electrons were already waiting in the wire — the generator just pushes them.
Human: how does a refrigerator work
Brain: It pumps heat out, not cold in. A compressor squeezes refrigerant gas until it's hot, coils on the back dump that heat to your kitchen, then the gas expands through a tiny valve into the freezer coils — expanding gas gets cold, absorbing heat from inside. The cycle repeats. Your fridge is a heat mover: the kitchen gets warmer so the inside stays cold. That is also exactly how air conditioners and heat pumps work.
Human: how does air conditioning work
Brain: Same as a fridge, bigger: refrigerant loops between indoor coils (cold, absorbing room heat) and outdoor coils (hot, dumping it outside). The compressor does the work, using electricity to move heat uphill. A heat pump is an AC with a reversing valve — in winter it steals heat from cold outside air and brings it in. Moving heat takes far less energy than making it, which is why heat pumps are 3-4x more efficient than heaters.
Human: how does a microwave oven heat food
Brain: Microwaves (2.45 GHz radio waves) make water, fat, and sugar molecules flip back and forth billions of times per second. The molecular friction becomes heat — inside the food, not from the outside in. That is why microwaves heat unevenly (cold spots where waves cancel) and why metal sparks (it concentrates the electric field). The mesh on the door blocks waves but lets you see — holes smaller than the wavelength.
Human: how does a washing machine work
Brain: Fill with water, add detergent (surfactants that lift dirt), then agitate — the drum or agitator sloshes clothes through soapy water. Drain, spin fast (centrifugal force flings water out through holes), rinse, spin again. Front-loaders tumble clothes through a puddle (less water, gentler); top-loaders swish them in a full tub. The spin cycle does the real drying work before the dryer even starts.
Human: how does a toilet flush
Brain: Gravity and siphons. The tank holds clean water; lifting the flapper dumps it fast into the bowl. The rush fills the bowl's S-shaped trap, and once water flows over the trap's hump, siphon action pulls everything down the drain. The tank refills via a float valve that shuts off at the right level. No pump, no power — 400-year-old physics (invented 1596) still in every bathroom.
Human: how does a lock and key work
Brain: A pin tumbler lock has a plug (rotates) inside a housing, with pin stacks blocking the shear line. Each pin pair has different lengths. The key's cuts lift each pair so the split between pins aligns exactly with the shear line — then the plug turns. Wrong key, pins block. Lockpicking lifts pins one by one by feel. It is a mechanical password.
Human: how does a zipper work
Brain: The slider is a Y-shaped channel: teeth enter as two rows and exit as one interlocked row. Each tooth has a tiny hook and hollow; the slider forces them to mesh alternately. Pull back and the channel's wedge shape pries them apart. A 1913 invention (Gideon Sundback) that replaced buttons for speed — geometry doing the work.
Human: how do elevators work safely
Brain: A motor winds steel cables over a pulley; a counterweight (about the car's weight plus half its max load) balances it so the motor only handles the difference. If cables fail, spring-loaded brakes wedge into the shaft rails — Elisha Otis demonstrated this in 1854 by cutting the rope on stage. Multiple cables each strong enough alone, plus the brake: elevators are among the safest transport ever.
Human: how does GPS find you, in detail
Brain: 24+ satellites orbit with atomic clocks, broadcasting their position and the exact time. Your receiver catches signals from at least 4. Each signal's travel time times light speed = your distance to that satellite. Three distances narrow you to two points; the fourth picks the right one and corrects your cheap clock. Result: position within meters, anywhere on Earth, free.
Human: how do noise cancelling headphones work
Brain: Destructive interference. A microphone samples outside noise; the headphone plays the exact inverse wave — peaks where noise has troughs. They cancel in your ear. It works best on steady drones (engines, AC) because the wave is predictable; sudden sounds are too fast to invert. The silence is manufactured, sample by sample.
Human: how does a camera capture a photo
Brain: A lens focuses light onto a sensor — millions of photosites, each counting photons. A color filter over each site (Bayer pattern: 2 green, 1 red, 1 blue per 4) means each pixel really measures one color; software interpolates the rest. The processor converts the raw counts into a JPEG: white balance, sharpening, compression. Your phone does this 30+ times a second for video.
Human: how does a 3D printer work
Brain: It builds objects layer by layer from a digital model. FDM printers melt plastic filament and draw each layer like a hot-glue gun on rails. Resin printers cure liquid plastic with UV light, layer by layer, for fine detail. Slicing software converts the 3D model into hundreds of thin toolpaths. Slow, but it makes shapes no factory can.
Human: how does a QR code work
Brain: A grid of black and white squares encoding data with heavy error correction — up to 30 percent can be damaged and it still scans. Three big corner squares tell the scanner its orientation and scale. Your camera finds the corners, un-warps the perspective, reads the bits, corrects errors, and decodes the text or URL. A barcode with a second dimension and a PhD.
Human: how does a smoke detector work
Brain: Two types. Ionization: a tiny bit of americium-241 emits particles that ionize air, creating a small current; smoke disrupts the current and the alarm sounds — best for fast flames. Photoelectric: an LED shines into a chamber; smoke scatters light onto a sensor — best for smoldering fires. Both are cheap, and the 3 AM low-battery chirp is by design: annoying enough that you actually change it.
Human: how does a lithium battery work
Brain: Lithium ions shuttle between graphite (anode) and metal oxide (cathode) through an electrolyte. Charging pushes ions to the anode; discharging lets them flow back, and electrons forced through your circuit on the way power your device. Each cycle grows tiny deposits and cracks — capacity fades. Heat accelerates aging; full charges stress it most. That is why phones last 2-3 years of daily cycles.
Human: how does a solar panel make electricity
Brain: Photons knock electrons loose in silicon. A solar cell is silicon doped two ways (n-type with extra electrons, p-type with holes) forming a junction with a built-in electric field. Knocked-loose electrons get swept one way by the field — current flows. One cell makes ~0.6 volts; panels wire dozens in series. Efficiency is 20-25 percent; the rest becomes heat. No moving parts, 25-year warranties.
Human: how does a wind turbine work
Brain: Wind pushes the blades — shaped like airplane wings, lift does the pulling, not just pushing. The rotor spins a shaft (often through a gearbox) driving a generator. Bigger blades and taller towers catch more wind (power scales with the cube of wind speed — double the wind, eight times the power). Pitch controls twist the blades to shed excess wind in storms.
Human: how does a computer work, part by part
Brain: The CPU fetches instructions from RAM, decodes them, executes in the ALU (arithmetic logic unit), and stores results — billions of times per second. RAM is fast temporary workspace (lost without power); SSD/storage is slow permanent memory. The motherboard connects everything via buses. The GPU is a second processor built for parallel math (graphics, AI). The OS (Windows, Linux) manages hardware and gives each program its own protected memory. Input becomes bits, bits become instructions, instructions become output.
Human: what is the difference between RAM and storage
Brain: RAM is the desk — fast, small, everything you're working on right now, wiped when power goes. Storage (SSD/HDD) is the filing cabinet — slow, huge, permanent. 16 GB RAM with a full desk slows down (swapping to storage); a full filing cabinet just means no room for new files. This is why more RAM helps multitasking but doesn't store anything.
Human: how does an operating system work
Brain: The OS is the computer's manager. It divides CPU time between programs (scheduling), gives each its own memory space (so one crash doesn't kill all), manages files on storage, and talks to hardware through drivers. When you double-click, the OS loads the program, gives it resources, and cleans up when it exits. Android, Windows, Linux — same job, different styles.
Human: how does the internet know where to send data
Brain: IP addresses and routers. Every packet carries a destination IP. Routers are intersection cops: each knows which direction gets packets closer, and forwards them. BGP, the internet's routing protocol, lets networks tell each other which IPs they can reach. Your packet may cross 15 routers and an ocean — each only knew the next hop, yet it arrives. No center, just cooperation.
Human: what is the cloud
Brain: Someone else's computer, rented by the minute. Your photos "in the cloud" sit on servers in a data center — giant buildings full of racks, cooling, and backup power. The magic is virtualization: one physical server pretends to be many, scaling up and down on demand. The cloud is real machines; the marketing just hides the address.
Human: how does email travel
Brain: You hit send; your app gives it to an SMTP server. DNS finds the recipient's mail server (MX records). The message hops across, gets scanned for spam and viruses, and lands in their inbox storage. IMAP lets you read it from anywhere without moving it. Email is 50-year-old tech — simple, open, and still unbeatable for its job.
Human: how does a search engine work
Brain: Three parts. Crawlers constantly download the web. An index maps every word to the pages containing it (like a book's index, planet-sized). When you search, the engine looks up your words and ranks pages by relevance signals — links, freshness, your location, hundreds of factors. Google's original insight: a link is a vote, and votes from trusted pages count more.
Human: how does video streaming work
Brain: The video is chopped into small segments in multiple qualities. Your player downloads a few seconds ahead, starting low-quality for instant playback, then stepping up as bandwidth allows (adaptive bitrate). CDNs — servers in your city — serve the segments so they travel short distances. Pause, and the buffer fills; your connection dips, and quality drops smoothly instead of freezing.
Human: how does a phone call work
Brain: Your voice becomes digital samples (8,000 per second for calls), compressed, and sent as packets over cellular or VoIP. Towers hand your moving phone from cell to cell. At the other end, packets become sound again. The slight delay on long calls is physics — signals crossing oceans and satellites take real milliseconds.
Human: how does Bluetooth work
Brain: Short-range radio (2.4 GHz) that pairs two devices with a handshake and encryption key. It hops frequencies 1,600 times per second to dodge interference. Classic Bluetooth streams audio; Bluetooth Low Energy sips power for watches and sensors. Range is ~10 meters by design — your headphones shouldn't reach the neighbor's phone.
Human: how does a digital payment work
Brain: Your card or phone sends a token (not your real number) to the terminal. The terminal asks your bank: is this valid, are funds available? The bank approves, the network (Visa/Mastercard) routes the guarantee, and settlement moves real money later. Tap-to-pay uses NFC radio plus one-time cryptograms — safer than the magnetic stripe it replaced.
Human: how does an ATM give you cash
Brain: Your card's chip proves your identity with the bank over an encrypted line. The bank approves; cassettes inside the ATM feed bills past sensors that check each note; the dispenser hands them out. The machine never "decides" — it is a robot arm following the bank's yes. Skimmers steal by copying your card data at the slot — wiggle the reader before inserting.
Human: how does a barcode scanner read
Brain: It shines light and measures reflection: black bars absorb, white spaces reflect. The pattern of widths encodes numbers. The laser sweeps or camera captures it, software matches the pattern to digits, and the store's database turns digits into a price. QR codes are the 2D upgrade — same idea, more data.
Human: how does a compass work
Brain: A magnetized needle aligns with Earth's magnetic field, pointing to magnetic north. The liquid inside steadies it. Near iron or electronics it lies — that is why phones calibrate with a figure-8 wave. Magnetic north drifts yearly; maps note the declination to true north. A 2,000-year-old Chinese invention, still in every survival kit.
Human: how does radar work
Brain: Shout radio waves and listen for the echo. The delay gives distance (waves travel at light speed), the frequency shift (Doppler) gives speed toward or away. Weather radar sees raindrops; police radar sees your car; air traffic radar sees planes. Bats invented it 50 million years earlier — sonar with sound.
Human: how does a thermostat control temperature
Brain: A sensor measures room temperature; you set a target. If the room is colder (heating mode), it switches the furnace on; at target, off. The small gap before switching back (hysteresis) stops rapid on-off cycling. Smart thermostats learn your schedule and pre-heat. It is a feedback loop — measure, compare, act — the same loop in cruise control and autopilots.
Human: how does cruise control work
Brain: You set a speed; wheel sensors report actual speed; the computer adjusts throttle to match. Uphill it adds fuel, downhill it eases off. Adaptive cruise adds radar: it slows to keep distance from the car ahead, then resumes. It is a simple feedback controller — the great-grandfather of self-driving.
Human: how does autopilot on planes work
Brain: It holds heading, altitude, and speed using gyroscopes, GPS, and air data. The pilot programs the route; the autopilot flies the boring straight parts with superhuman steadiness. Pilots still handle takeoff, landing, and anything unusual. Autopilot doesn't replace pilots — it replaces fatigue.
Human: how does a vending machine work
Brain: You pay; it validates coins/bills or card. You pick; a controller runs a motor turning a spiral coil, pushing one item forward until it drops. Sensors confirm the drop (or refund). Refrigerated ones are just fridges with a robot arm. The whole business is location plus reliability — a full machine in a busy spot prints money.
Human: how does a traffic light decide when to change
Brain: Simple timers on quiet roads; induction loops (wire coils under the asphalt detecting your car's metal) or cameras on smart ones. The controller runs phases: green waves timed so steady drivers hit every green. Emergency vehicles can preempt with strobe sensors. Bad timing wastes millions of human-hours — traffic engineering is applied patience.
Human: how does a water treatment plant clean water
Brain: Coagulation (chemicals clump dirt), sedimentation (clumps settle), filtration (sand and charcoal catch the rest), disinfection (chlorine or UV kills germs), then fluoride and pH adjustment. Your tap water went through this in hours. Then pumps push it uphill to towers, and gravity delivers pressure to your home.
Human: how does a sewage plant work
Brain: Screens catch trash, settling tanks let solids sink (sludge), bacteria eat dissolved waste in aeration tanks (they get oxygen bubbled in), more settling, then disinfection before release to rivers. The sludge gets digested for biogas. It is a managed ecosystem — bacteria doing what they've done for billions of years, at city scale.
Human: how does a dam make electricity
Brain: Water stored high has potential energy. Gates release it through penstocks to turbines at the bottom — falling water spins the turbine, the turbine spins the generator. More height and flow means more power. Dams also store energy: open the gates when demand peaks. The Hoover Dam's lake took years to fill and powers a million people.
Human: how does a nuclear power plant work
Brain: Uranium atoms split (fission), releasing heat. The heat boils water into steam; steam spins a turbine; the turbine spins a generator — the same ending as a coal plant, different fire. Control rods absorb neutrons to throttle the reaction. Cooling towers dump waste heat as steam clouds. The fuel is tiny (a golf ball of uranium powers your life), the concrete and caution are enormous.
Human: how does a gas furnace heat a home
Brain: The thermostat calls; gas flows to burners and ignites; a heat exchanger (metal box) gets hot without letting exhaust mix with air; a blower pushes house air across the exchanger and through ducts. Exhaust vents outside. A flame sensor confirms fire — no flame, no gas (safety). Annual servicing keeps the exchanger crack-free; cracks leak carbon monoxide.
Human: how does a jet engine work
Brain: Suck, squeeze, bang, blow. A fan sucks in huge air volumes; the compressor squeezes it; fuel burns in the compressed air; the blast shoots out the back, pushing the plane forward (Newton's third law). The turbine in the exhaust stream powers the compressor. A 747's engines each make 60,000+ pounds of thrust by moving tons of air per second.
Human: how does an LED bulb make light
Brain: Electricity pushes electrons across a semiconductor junction; when they drop into holes, they release the energy as photons — direct electricity to light, no heat waste like old bulbs. Different semiconductor materials make different colors; white LEDs are blue LEDs plus yellow phosphor coating. That is why LEDs use 85 percent less power than incandescents: almost no energy becomes heat.
Human: how does a bicycle work
Brain: Your legs are the engine: pedals turn a chainring, the chain drives the rear sprocket, gears trade force for speed. The frame is a triangle — the strongest shape. Gyroscopic effect and trail (fork angle) keep it stable at speed; at slow speed you balance actively. Brakes squeeze the rim or a disc. A bike is 95 percent efficient at turning your energy into motion — the most efficient transport ever invented.
Human: how does a helicopter fly
Brain: The main rotor is a spinning wing — its blades are airfoils, and tilting them (cyclic pitch) makes lift stronger on one side, tipping the helicopter that way. The tail rotor counters the torque that would spin the body opposite the blades. Collective pitch changes all blades' angle at once for up/down. It can hover because it makes its own wind — at the cost of complexity and noise.
Human: how does a submarine dive and surface
Brain: Ballast tanks. Flood them with water and the sub gets heavy and sinks; blow compressed air in to push water out and it floats. Fine trim with smaller tanks and dive planes (underwater wings). Nuclear subs make their own air and water, staying down for months — the limit is food and crew sanity, not technology.
Human: how does a clock keep time
Brain: Something that oscillates steadily, counted. Pendulum clocks: a swinging weight (period set by length). Quartz watches: a crystal vibrating 32,768 times per second when electrified, counted down to one tick per second. Atomic clocks: cesium atoms resonating at exactly 9,192,631,770 wiggles per second — so steady they define the second itself. Better oscillator, better clock.
Human: how does a printer print
Brain: Laser: a laser draws your page on a charged drum, toner (plastic powder) sticks to the charged drawing, heat fuses it to paper — fast and crisp. Inkjet: tiny nozzles spit droplets (thousands per second) where the page needs color — cheaper, slower. Both are just precise ways to put dots exactly where software says.
Human: how does a projector show movies
Brain: A bright lamp shines through (or reflects off) a chip with millions of tiny mirrors or liquid-crystal shutters — one per pixel — that open and close to shape the light into your image, then a lens throws it big onto the screen. DLP uses mirrors, LCD uses shutters, laser projectors use pure colored lasers. 24 pictures a second plus persistence of vision equals motion.
Human: how do glasses fix vision
Brain: They pre-bend light to compensate your eye's error. Nearsighted eyes focus too strongly (image lands before the retina) — concave lenses spread light to push focus back. Farsighted eyes focus weakly — convex lenses add bending. The prescription numbers are just how much bending, in diopters, and in which direction (astigmatism). Lenses are custom optics for your personal eyeball defect.
Human: how does a stethoscope work
Brain: It just carries sound well. The chest piece's diaphragm vibrates with body sounds; the sealed tubes guide the vibrations to your ears with little loss. Two ears (binaural) help locate the sound. Doctors learn the library: normal lub-dub, murmurs of leaky valves, crackles of fluid in lungs. A 200-year-old design (invented 1816) that still beats gadgets for speed.
Human: how does an X-ray see bones
Brain: X-rays are high-energy light that passes through soft tissue but gets absorbed by dense calcium in bones. Film or a digital detector behind you records the shadows: bones white, tissue gray, air black. Lead aprons block the rays where you don't need them. CT scans rotate the X-ray around you and compute a 3D image from hundreds of shadows.
Human: how does ultrasound see inside the body
Brain: It shouts sound too high to hear and listens for echoes. Different tissues echo differently; timing gives depth. The computer paints the echoes into an image in real time — that is why pregnancy scans move. No radiation, safe and cheap, but it can't see through bone or air well. Bats and dolphins had it first.
Human: how does a fire extinguisher work
Brain: It removes one side of the fire triangle (heat, fuel, oxygen). Water cools (not for grease or electrical fires). CO2 smothers oxygen and leaves no mess — good for electronics. Dry chemical coats fuel and interrupts the chemistry — the red canister everywhere. PASS: Pull pin, Aim low, Squeeze, Sweep. And know when to leave — extinguishers are for small fires, not heroics.
Human: how does a parachute slow you down
Brain: Air resistance. The canopy catches air, and drag grows with the square of speed — fall fast, get pushed hard, until drag equals weight and you descend steadily (~20 km/h for a person). Ram-air parachutes are wings you can steer. Reserve chutes are simpler and packed to open no matter what. Packing it right is literally life or death — riggers log every pack.
Human: how does a sailboat move against the wind
Brain: The sail is a vertical wing: wind flowing across it creates lift pulling the boat forward-ish. The keel (underwater fin) stops sideways slide, so the forward component wins. Tacking zigzags upwind — never straight, but always progressing. Sailors read wind like a language: lulls, gusts, shifts.
Human: how does a rocket nozzle work
Brain: It converts hot high-pressure gas into fast directional exhaust. The throat chokes flow to Mach 1, then the bell expands it — gas speeds up as pressure drops, reaching Mach 3-5 at the exit. Bigger expansion for vacuum (no air to push against), smaller for sea level. The shape is pure physics: every curve is calculated for maximum push per fuel.
Human: how does a touchscreen glove work
Brain: Conductive thread in the fingertips carries your finger's electric field to the screen. Regular gloves insulate; touchscreen gloves are wired through. The whole glove doesn't need it — just the contact points. Simple, and the reason cheap gloves fail (too little conductive area).
Human: how does a power bank charge your phone
Brain: It is a big lithium battery with USB ports and a boost circuit stepping its 3.7 volts up to 5 volts USB. You charge the bank from the wall, then it charges your phone later — energy stored, energy moved. Capacity in mAh is at 3.7V, so a 10,000 mAh bank gives ~6,500 mAh at 5V after conversion losses. Marketing numbers vs physics numbers.
Human: how does a surge protector work
Brain: A component (MOV) sits across the power line doing nothing — until voltage spikes, when it suddenly conducts and dumps the excess to ground, sacrificing itself bit by bit. The joule rating is its lifetime budget; after big surges it's used up (the "protected" light goes out). It guards against spikes, not blackouts or lightning strikes — those need whole-house protection.
Human: how does double glazing keep heat in
Brain: Two panes with a gap (often argon gas) between. Heat crosses by conduction through glass, convection in air, and radiation. The gap kills convection (thin gap, heavy gas barely moves), low-E coatings reflect radiant heat back inside. Two panes roughly halve heat loss vs one. Triple glazing halves it again — diminishing returns, like most insulation.
