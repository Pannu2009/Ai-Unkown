SIKH HISTORY IN DEPTH

THE TEN GURUS

Q: Who was Guru Nanak and when did he live?
A: Guru Nanak (1469-1539) was the founder of Sikhism, born in Talwandi, now Nankana Sahib in Pakistan. He rejected both empty ritual in Hinduism and Islam of his time and taught Ik Onkar, that there is one God. His core teaching was Naam Japo (meditate on God's name), Kirat Karo (earn an honest living), and Vand Chhako (share what you earn). He composed the Japji Sahib, the opening prayer of the Guru Granth Sahib, and traveled on four long journeys called Udasis across South Asia, Tibet, and Arabia. He appointed his disciple Lehna as successor, renaming him Angad.

Q: Who was Guru Angad and what was his contribution?
A: Guru Angad (1504-1552), born Lehna in Matte-di-Sarai, became the second Guru in 1539 at Khadur Sahib. His greatest contribution was standardizing the Gurmukhi script, giving the Punjabi language a written form that ordinary people could read. He also formalized the langar tradition of free community meals and emphasized physical fitness alongside spiritual life, starting wrestling traditions. He compiled Guru Nanak's writings.

Q: Who was Guru Amar Das and what did he establish?
A: Guru Amar Das (1479-1574) became the third Guru at age 73 and led from Goindwal. He organized Sikhism into 22 missionary districts called Manjis, each led by a preacher. He made langar mandatory before any audience with him, forcing even Emperor Akbar to sit and eat with commoners, which struck at the caste system. He banned the practices of sati and purdah among Sikhs and composed the Anand Sahib, the Song of Bliss. He also established the city of Goindwal with its famous baoli, a stepped well with 84 steps.

Q: Who was Guru Ram Das and what did he found?
A: Guru Ram Das (1534-1581), the fourth Guru, founded the city of Ramdaspur, which became Amritsar. He dug the holy tank Amrit Sarovar, the Pool of Nectar. He composed the Lavan, the four hymns sung during the Sikh wedding ceremony called Anand Karaj. He also organized Sikh congregations into a structured community with collectors called Masands.

Q: Who was Guru Arjan and why is he central to Sikh history?
A: Guru Arjan (1563-1606), the fifth Guru, built the Harmandir Sahib, the Golden Temple, in the middle of the Amrit Sarovar, with four doors open to all four castes and directions. In 1604 he compiled the Adi Granth, the first version of the Sikh scripture, installing it in the Harmandir Sahib with Baba Buddha as the first Granthi. He was arrested on orders of Emperor Jahangir and tortured to death in Lahore in 1606, becoming the first Sikh martyr, Shaheed. His martyrdom marked the turning point where Sikhism took up arms in self-defense.

Q: Who was Guru Hargobind and what is Miri-Piri?
A: Guru Hargobind (1595-1644), the sixth Guru, was only 11 when his father Guru Arjan was martyred. He wore two swords, Miri and Piri, symbolizing temporal and spiritual authority, declaring that spiritual life and the defense of the innocent go together. In 1609 he built the Akal Takht, the Throne of the Timeless, facing the Harmandir Sahib as the seat of Sikh political authority. He raised an army, fought defensive battles against Mughal forces, and was imprisoned in Gwalior Fort, where he secured the release of 52 Hindu princes with him, earning the name Bandi Chhor, the liberator.

Q: Who was Guru Har Rai?
A: Guru Har Rai (1630-1661), the seventh Guru, led from Kiratpur. He was known for compassion toward all living things and kept a large army as his grandfather had instructed, though he avoided conflict. He maintained herbal gardens for medicine and helped the sick. He died young at 31.

Q: Who was Guru Har Krishan?
A: Guru Har Krishan (1656-1664), the eighth Guru, became Guru at age 5. When smallpox struck Delhi, he served the sick personally without fear and died of the disease himself at age 8 in 1664. He is remembered as Bal Guru, the child Guru, and his shrine in Delhi is Gurdwara Bangla Sahib.

Q: Who was Guru Tegh Bahadur and why was he martyred?
A: Guru Tegh Bahadur (1621-1675), the ninth Guru, was martyred in Delhi on November 11, 1675, on the orders of Emperor Aurangzeb. Kashmiri Pandits had come to him for help against forced conversion to Islam, and he chose to sacrifice his own life to defend their right to practice their religion. He was beheaded at Chandni Chowk, and the site is marked by Gurdwara Sis Ganj Sahib. He is called Hind Di Chadar, the protective shield of India, because he died defending not his own faith but the religious freedom of others. His companion Bhai Mati Das was sawn alive for refusing to convert.

Q: Who was Guru Gobind Singh and what did he create?
A: Guru Gobind Singh (1666-1708), the tenth Guru, born Gobind Rai in Patna, transformed the Sikhs into the Khalsa on Vaisakhi 1699 at Anandpur Sahib. He asked for five volunteers willing to give their heads, the Panj Pyare, the Five Beloved, and baptized them with Amrit, sweetened water stirred with a double-edged sword. He gave them the Five Ks: Kesh (uncut hair), Kangha (comb), Kara (steel bracelet), Kachera (undergarment), and Kirpan (sword). He declared that the line of personal Gurus would end with him and that the Guru Granth Sahib would be the eternal Guru. He was assassinated at Nanded in 1708. His sons, the Chaar Sahibzade, were all martyred: the elder two, Ajit Singh (18) and Jujhar Singh (14), died fighting at Chamkaur in 1704, and the younger two, Zorawar Singh (9) and Fateh Singh (7), were bricked alive at Sirhind for refusing to convert.

THE FIVE TAKHTS

Q: What are the Five Takhts?
A: The Five Takhts are the five seats of Sikh temporal authority. The Akal Takht at Amritsar, founded by Guru Hargobind in 1609, is the supreme throne facing the Golden Temple. Takht Sri Keshgarh Sahib at Anandpur Sahib is where the Khalsa was born in 1699. Takht Sri Damdama Sahib at Talwandi Sabo is where Guru Gobind Singh compiled the final version of the Guru Granth Sahib in 1705. Takht Sri Patna Sahib in Bihar marks the birthplace of Guru Gobind Singh. Takht Sri Hazur Sahib at Nanded in Maharashtra is where Guru Gobind Singh passed away in 1708. Edicts called Hukamnamas issued from the Akal Takht carry authority for the whole Sikh community.

MAJOR BATTLES

Q: What happened at the Battle of Chamkaur in 1704?
A: In December 1704, Guru Gobind Singh with about 40 Sikhs was besieged in a mud house at Chamkaur by a Mughal army numbering in the tens of thousands. The Sikhs fought in batches of five, and all 40 died fighting. The Guru's elder sons Ajit Singh, aged 18, and Jujhar Singh, aged 14, both fell in the battle after asking their father's permission to fight. The Guru escaped at night disguised, guided by three surviving Sikhs. Chamkaur is remembered as the ultimate stand of courage against impossible odds, and the 40 are honored as martyrs.

Q: What happened at Muktsar in 1705?
A: At Khidrana, now Muktsar, in 1705, the 40 Sikhs who had deserted Guru Gobind Singh at Anandpur returned under Mai Bhago to block the pursuing Mughal army. All 40 died fighting and were blessed by the Guru as the Chali Mukte, the Forty Liberated Ones. Their act of returning to die for the cause redeemed their earlier desertion, and Muktsar means the Pool of Liberation. Mai Bhago survived and became one of the Guru's most trusted companions.

Q: What were the sieges of Anandpur?
A: Anandpur Sahib, founded by Guru Tegh Bahadur, was besieged multiple times by Mughal forces and allied hill rajas between 1700 and 1704. The longest siege starved the Sikh defenders until the Mughals swore on the Quran and the hill rajas on their sacred texts that the Sikhs would have safe passage if they left. The oaths were broken immediately: the evacuating column was attacked at the Sarsa river in December 1704, families were separated, and the events led directly to Chamkaur and the martyrdom of the younger Sahibzadas at Sirhind.

BANDA SINGH BAHADUR

Q: Who was Banda Singh Bahadur?
A: Banda Singh Bahadur (1670-1716), born Lachman Dev in Kashmir, was a Bairagi ascetic converted by Guru Gobind Singh at Nanded in 1708 and sent to Punjab with five arrows and a battle standard. He raised an army of peasants and within two years sacked Sirhind in 1710, executing Wazir Khan, the governor who had ordered the young Sahibzadas bricked alive. He established the first Sikh rule, minted coins in the name of the Guru, and abolished the zamindari system, giving land to the tillers. Captured in 1715 after the siege of Gurdas Nangal, he was taken to Delhi in chains and tortured to death in 1716, refusing to convert to Islam. His son's heart was cut out before his eyes; he did not flinch.

THE SIKH EMPIRE

Q: Who was Maharaja Ranjit Singh?
A: Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780-1839), called Sher-e-Punjab, the Lion of Punjab, united the Sikh misls and founded the Sikh Empire in 1799 with Lahore as his capital. At its height the empire stretched from the Khyber Pass to Kashmir and from Multan to the Sutlej. He built a modern army with French and Italian generals, kept the empire secular with Muslim, Hindu, and Sikh ministers, and never allowed the death penalty. He took the Koh-i-Noor diamond from Shah Shuja of Afghanistan. He died in 1839, and the empire fell to the British after the two Anglo-Sikh wars of 1845-46 and 1848-49, ending with the annexation of Punjab in 1849 and the exile of the boy king Duleep Singh.

GURDWARA REFORM MOVEMENT

Q: What was the Gurdwara Reform Movement?
A: Between 1920 and 1925, Sikhs organized to free their Gurdwaras from corrupt hereditary priests called Mahants who had taken control during the 1700s. The movement created the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, the SGPC, in 1920 to manage the shrines democratically. The British-backed Mahants resisted violently. In February 1921, over 130 Sikhs were massacred at Nankana Sahib by Mahant Narain Das's men, an event that shocked India and forced the British to pass the Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925, handing control of the shrines to elected Sikh bodies. The Akali movement's nonviolent marches, the Jathas, became a model of disciplined protest.

PARTITION 1947

Q: What happened to Sikhs during Partition in 1947?
A: The partition of Punjab in 1947 split the Sikh homeland in two. Sikhs had to abandon Nankana Sahib, the birthplace of Guru Nanak, Kartarpur, and hundreds of historic Gurdwaras that fell in Pakistan. Around a million people died in the communal violence, and Sikhs suffered terribly in the riots of Rawalpindi, Multan, and Lahore. About 2.5 million Sikhs migrated east to Indian Punjab, Delhi, and beyond as refugees. The Radcliffe Line cut through villages, farms, and families overnight. The trauma of 1947 is burned into Sikh memory and explains the deep attachment to the historic shrines now across the border.

1984 AND AFTERMATH

Q: What happened in 1984?
A: In June 1984, the Indian Army launched Operation Blue Star on the Golden Temple complex to remove the militant leader Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale and his armed followers. The operation was timed on a major Sikh religious day when thousands of pilgrims were present, and the Akal Takht was heavily damaged by tank fire. Hundreds died, including many pilgrims. On October 31, 1984, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi was assassinated by her Sikh bodyguards in revenge. What followed in November 1984 were organized anti-Sikh pogroms in Delhi and other cities in which over 3,000 Sikhs were murdered, with mobs using voter lists to find Sikh homes. Very few perpetrators were ever convicted. The decade after saw an armed insurgency in Punjab and a brutal police crackdown. For Sikhs worldwide, 1984 remains an open wound and the defining event of the modern diaspora's political consciousness.

THE DIASPORA

Q: Where did Sikhs settle abroad?
A: Sikh migration began with soldiers and laborers in the British Empire. Early settlers worked on railways in Canada and farms in California in the 1900s; the Komagata Maru incident of 1914, when Canada turned away a ship of Indian passengers, became a landmark of racist exclusion. After the 1960s, large communities grew in the UK, especially Southall in London, and in Canada, especially Surrey and Brampton. The United States, Australia, and New Zealand followed. Today there are about 25 to 30 million Sikhs worldwide, with over 800,000 in Canada alone. The diaspora built hundreds of Gurdwaras abroad, and Sikh soldiers, farmers, truckers, and professionals became visible across the West. After 1984, the diaspora became the keeper of Sikh political memory and the main funder of Punjab's institutions.

KEY GURBANI CONCEPTS

Q: What is Hukam?
A: Hukam is the divine order or will that runs the universe. Guru Nanak opens the Japji Sahib with the question of how to become truthful and how to break the wall of falsehood, and answers: by walking in Hukam. Living in Hukam means accepting what comes with grace instead of fighting reality with ego. It is not passive resignation; it is aligning your will with the way things are, like a river flowing with its banks instead of against them. A Sikh who lives in Hukam faces joy and sorrow with the same steady mind.

Q: What is Seva?
A: Seva is selfless service done without desire for reward or recognition. It is one of the three pillars of Sikh life. Seva takes many forms: serving food in the langar, cleaning the Gurdwara, helping the sick, defending the helpless. The key is Nishkam, without motive; service done for praise or social media is not Seva in the Gurbani sense. The langar, where everyone sits on the floor and eats the same food, is Seva made visible: it erases caste, wealth, and status three times a day.

Q: What is Simran?
A: Simran is the remembrance of God's name, the practice of keeping the mind anchored in Naam. It can be spoken aloud, whispered, or done silently with the breath. Guru Nanak taught that Simran washes the mind the way soap washes cloth. The goal is not mechanical repetition but Ajapa Jap, the state where remembrance continues by itself, like a heartbeat. Sikhs practice Simran in the early morning hours called Amrit Vela, before dawn, when the world is quiet.

Q: What are the three pillars of Sikh life?
A: Naam Japo, meditate on God's name; Kirat Karo, earn an honest living by your own effort; Vand Chhako, share what you earn with others. These three commands from Guru Nanak cover the spiritual, economic, and social life in one framework. A Sikh should not beg, should not steal, should not exploit, and should not eat alone while others hunger.

Q: What are the Five Vices?
A: The Five Thieves that rob the mind are Kam (lust and unchecked desire), Krodh (anger and rage), Lobh (greed and hoarding), Moh (attachment that blinds judgment), and Ahankar (ego and pride). Gurbani describes them as thieves that break into the house of the mind. They are not defeated by suppression but by redirecting the mind toward Naam and Seva; a mind full of remembrance has no room for thieves.

Q: What are the Five Virtues?
A: Against the five thieves stand five virtues: Sat (truthfulness), Santokh (contentment), Daya (compassion), Nimrata (humility), and Pyaar (love). Santokh is especially prized: the contented person is richer than the king who wants more. Nimrata, humility, is called the true strength, because the humble bent grass survives the storm that breaks the proud tree.

Q: What is the Guru Granth Sahib?
A: The Guru Granth Sahib is the Sikh scripture and the eternal living Guru, as declared by Guru Gobind Singh in 1708. It contains 1430 pages of hymns by the Sikh Gurus, 15 Hindu Bhagats like Kabir and Ravidas, 11 Bhatts, and 4 Sikhs including Baba Farid. It is written in Gurmukhi script but includes many languages: Punjabi, Hindi, Persian, Sanskrit, and regional dialects. It is organized by musical measures called Raags, 31 in total, because the hymns are meant to be sung, not just read. Every Gurdwara keeps it on a throne under a canopy, and it is treated with the respect due to a living teacher: fanned with a chauri, put to rest at night in the Sukhasan ceremony, and woken in the morning at Prakash.

Q: What is the Khalsa code of conduct?
A: The Rehat Maryada, the Sikh code of conduct, requires the baptized Khalsa Sikh to keep the Five Ks at all times, rise early for prayers, meditate on Naam, earn honestly, share with the needy, and never cut the hair. It forbids four cardinal sins called Bajjar Kurehits: cutting hair, eating halal meat, adultery, and using tobacco or intoxicants. A Sikh who breaks one must present themselves before the congregation and be rebaptized. The code also forbids caste discrimination: all Sikhs share the names Singh and Kaur, lion and princess, given at baptism in 1699.

Q: What is Vaisakhi?
A: Vaisakhi, celebrated on April 13 or 14, is the Sikh harvest festival and the birthday of the Khalsa. In 1699 Guru Gobind Singh chose this day at Anandpur Sahib to create the Khalsa, and every year since, Sikhs gather at Gurdwaras, hold Nagar Kirtan processions through the streets, and many choose Vaisakhi day to take Amrit baptism. In Punjab the wheat harvest is coming in, so the festival joins the spiritual and the agricultural year: thanksgiving for the crop and renewal of the community's vows.

Q: What is the meaning of Ik Onkar?
A: Ik Onkar, written as ੴ, is the opening of the Guru Granth Sahib and the foundation of Sikh theology: there is one God, one creative reality behind all existence. The numeral one joined to the word Onkar, the primal sound, declares absolute monotheism without images, incarnations, or intermediaries. Every Sikh prayer begins with it, and it hangs in Gurdwaras and Sikh homes as the simplest possible statement of faith: one.

SIKH MUSIC AND THE RAAGS

Q: What is Kirtan?
A: Kirtan is the singing of Gurbani hymns in congregation, the central act of Sikh worship. The Guru Granth Sahib is organized by 31 Raags, musical measures, because the Gurus intended the scripture to be sung, not merely read. Traditional instruments are the harmonium, tabla, and the stringed instruments of the Gurus' time: the rabab, saranda, and dilruba. Guru Nanak's companion Bhai Mardana played the rabab, and Sikh musicians called Ragis train for years in the classical tradition. A Gurdwara service flows as Kirtan, then Katha (discourse), then Ardaas (prayer), then the distribution of Karah Parshad, the blessed pudding.

Q: What is the significance of the Raag system?
A: Each Raag is a musical framework tied to a mood and time of day. Raag Asa carries heroic and hopeful themes; Raag Sorath carries devotion; Raag Dhanasari is sung in the evening. The Gurus composed specific hymns in specific Raags because the music itself teaches: the same words in a morning Raag and a night Raag land differently on the heart. This makes the Guru Granth Sahib the largest collection of devotional poetry set to classical music in the world.

FESTIVALS

Q: What are the major Sikh festivals?
A: Vaisakhi in April marks the Khalsa's birth and the harvest. Gurpurabs celebrate the births and martyrdoms of the Gurus: Guru Nanak's Gurpurab in November is the largest, with three days of continuous Akhand Path readings of the whole scripture. Hola Mohalla, created by Guru Gobind Singh at Anandpur, is the Sikh festival of martial arts and mock battles, celebrated the day after Holi. Bandi Chhor Divas, the day of liberation, coincides with Diwali and celebrates Guru Hargobind's release from Gwalior with 52 princes. Maghi in January commemorates the 40 Mukte of Muktsar with a great fair.

SIKH MARTIAL HISTORY

Q: What is the Battle of Saragarhi?
A: On September 12, 1897, 21 Sikh soldiers of the British Indian Army held the signaling post of Saragarhi in the North-West Frontier against an estimated 10,000 Afghan tribesmen. They fought to the last man, and the last defender, Gurmukh Singh, kept signaling until he was killed. The British built a memorial gurdwara and awarded all 21 the Indian Order of Merit. It is studied in military academies as one of history's greatest last stands, and September 12 is commemorated by Sikhs worldwide.

Q: What was the Sikh role in the World Wars?
A: Sikhs served in enormous numbers in both World Wars. In World War I, over 130,000 Sikh soldiers fought for the British Empire in France, Gallipoli, and Mesopotamia. In World War II, Sikh regiments fought in North Africa, Italy, Burma, and Malaya, winning a large share of the Victoria Crosses awarded to Indian troops. The distinctive turbaned Sikh soldier became a symbol of courage. After the wars, many veterans settled in Britain and Canada, seeding the diaspora communities.

SIKH WOMEN IN HISTORY

Q: Who was Mai Bhago?
A: Mai Bhago was the Sikh warrior woman who shamed the 40 deserters at Anandpur into returning to fight at Muktsar in 1705, leading them herself into battle. She survived and became Guru Gobind Singh's bodyguard, one of the few to be so honored. She represents the Khalsa ideal that courage has no gender, and Sikh girls are taught her story alongside the battles.

Q: Who was Maharani Jind Kaur?
A: Maharani Jind Kaur (1817-1863), the youngest wife of Maharaja Ranjit Singh and mother of Duleep Singh, became regent of the Sikh Empire in 1843. After the British annexed Punjab in 1849, they feared her influence, imprisoned her, and exiled her to Nepal and then London, separating her from her son for 13 years. She is called the Messalina of Punjab by the British who feared her, and remembered by Sikhs as the last sovereign of the empire who never bowed.

Q: Who was Bibi Sharan Kaur?
A: During the 1705 siege of Chamkaur's aftermath traditions, Bibi Sharan Kaur is remembered as the woman who performed the cremation rites of the martyred elder Sahibzadas when none dared. Sikh tradition honors the women who preserved the community's dead and its memory through the darkest persecutions.

GURDWARA ETIQUETTE

Q: What are the rules for visiting a Gurdwara?
A: Cover your head with a scarf or the provided bandanas; remove your shoes and wash your hands and feet at the entrance; dress modestly; never carry tobacco or alcohol inside; bow before the Guru Granth Sahib and sit on the floor facing it, never with your feet pointed at the scripture; accept Karah Parshad with both hands cupped; and sit in the langar hall to eat the free community meal, which is offered to everyone regardless of religion. Photography of the scripture is discouraged. These rules apply equally to Sikhs and visitors, and Gurdwaras welcome all.

Q: What is Karah Parshad?
A: Karah Parshad is the blessed pudding of wheat flour, sugar, and ghee, cooked in an iron karahi and distributed to the congregation after prayers. Everyone receives an equal portion in cupped hands, king and beggar alike. It is sanctified by the recitation of Gurbani during its preparation and by the Ardaas prayer. Refusing it is considered disrespectful; it is the tangible taste of equality.

THE SIKH SYMBOLS

Q: What is the Khanda symbol?
A: The Khanda is the emblem of the Khalsa: a double-edged sword in the center, ringed by a chakkar (the circular throwing weapon representing the unity and eternity of God), flanked by two crossed kirpans. It appears on the Nishan Sahib, the saffron Sikh flag flown at every Gurdwara, and represents the union of spiritual and temporal power, Miri-Piri, in one mark.

THE KHALSA INITIATION

Q: What happens in the Amrit ceremony?
A: The Amrit Sanchar, the baptism of the Khalsa, is conducted by five initiated Sikhs representing the original Panj Pyare. Sugar crystals called patashas are dissolved in water in an iron bowl and stirred with a double-edged khanda while the five recite five prayers: Japji Sahib, Jaap Sahib, Tav Prasad Savaiye, Benti Chaupai, and Anand Sahib. Each candidate drinks the Amrit, has it sprinkled on the eyes and hair, and sips from the same bowl as all the others, erasing caste in one act. They are given the names Singh or Kaur and accept the discipline of the Rehat. Taking Amrit is a voluntary vow, often done at Vaisakhi, and it binds the Sikh to the Khalsa code for life.

SIKH CORE BELIEFS SUMMARY

Q: What do Sikhs believe about God?
A: Sikhs believe in one formless, timeless, all-pervading God who is both transcendent and within creation. God is not male or female, has no image or idol, and is reached through devotion, honest living, and service, not through ritual, pilgrimage, fasting, or priests. The Mool Mantar, the root verse at the start of the Guru Granth Sahib, defines God as one, truth, the creator, without fear, without hatred, timeless, unborn, self-existent, known by the Guru's grace.

Q: What do Sikhs believe about the afterlife?
A: Sikhism teaches reincarnation: the soul cycles through births until it merges with God, like a drop returning to the ocean. Liberation, Mukti, comes not from rituals but from living in Hukam, remembering Naam, and dissolving the ego. Heaven and hell are states of mind: union with God is heaven, separation is hell, both experienced here as well as hereafter.

Q: What is the Sikh view of other religions?
A: Guru Granth Sahib includes hymns of Hindu Bhagats and Muslim Sufis, declaring that the divine light is the same in all. Guru Tegh Bahadur died defending the religious freedom of Hindus. Sikhs are taught to respect all faiths while following their own path with discipline. Forced conversion is forbidden; sharing the faith is done by example, not pressure.

MODERN PUNJAB

Q: What is Punjab like today?
A: Indian Punjab is India's breadbasket, the heart of the Green Revolution, producing a large share of the country's wheat and rice. Punjabi is the official language, written in Gurmukhi. The state lives with the memory of the 1980s insurgency and the drug crisis that followed, problems Punjabi writers and singers confront openly. Pakistani Punjab, across the border, holds the Sikh holy sites of Nankana Sahib and Kartarpur, and the Kartarpur Corridor opened in 2019 lets Indian Sikhs visit Guru Nanak's final resting place without a visa. The dream of easy access to the historic Gurdwaras unites Sikhs on both sides.

Q: What is the SGPC?
A: The Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, formed in 1920 during the Gurdwara reform movement, is the elected body that manages the historic Sikh shrines in Punjab, including the Golden Temple. It runs schools, hospitals, and universities, publishes religious literature, and issues the Sikh Rehat Maryada. Its elections are among the largest religious elections in the world, and control of the SGPC is the center of Sikh institutional politics.

NOTABLE SIKHS

Q: Who are some notable Sikhs in history?
A: Maharaja Ranjit Singh built an empire. Bhagat Singh, though an atheist revolutionary, came from a Sikh family and is Punjab's beloved martyr. Dr. Manmohan Singh became India's Prime Minister in 2004, the first Sikh to hold the office. Milkha Singh, the Flying Sikh, was India's greatest sprinter. Harbhajan Singh and modern cricketers carried Sikh pride onto the world stage. In the diaspora, Sikhs serve as members of parliament in Canada and the UK, as police officers, soldiers, and judges, often while keeping the turban and beard in uniform, a right won through long legal battles.
